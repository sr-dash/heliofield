The Sun's rotation axis always points up the screen. Three preset views sit in the menu's View section:

  • From Earth looks at the Sun along the Sun–Earth line, as a ground telescope would see it.
  • From north looks down on the solar north pole, with Earth towards the top of the screen.
  • Whole fleet zooms out until every planet and spacecraft is in view.

A preset view follows Earth as you change dates. Once you drag the scene, the view stays where you left it.

Reset view. The round button at the lower right of the scene (or H) brings the camera back to the opening view: centred on the Sun, from Earth's side and a little above the ecliptic. It undoes any rotation, zoom and pan without reloading the page, so a field computed in the browser is kept.

Dates and sources

The date field opens a calendar for any day from 1 May 2010, the start of the SDO/HMI era, to today. Latest returns to the newest daily product. The arrows step one day at a time.

Recent days have a daily product, computed on the server each night from the last magnetogram of the day. They open at once. Daily products are kept for about four months, then one day per Carrington rotation.

Any other date is computed in your browser. The page asks first, because this replaces the field on screen. See Past dates for how it works.

The badge beside the date names the magnetogram behind the field:

  • GONG: the NSO Global Oscillation Network Group's zero-point-corrected synoptic map, the normal source for daily products.
  • HMI: the SDO/HMI daily synoptic frame, used for a daily product when GONG could not be reached at build time.
  • HMI CR n with a dashed outline: a date computed in your browser from the HMI map of Carrington rotation n.

Themes

The sun-and-moon button in the top bar switches between a dark and a light theme, and T does the same. The first visit follows your system setting, and after that the page remembers your choice. Links and embeds can fix it with theme=dark or theme=light.

The scene uses the same colour for each role in both themes; only lightness changes. See Colours and accessibility.

A tight cloud means a robust connection; a cloud split between two regions means the source is uncertain. See solar-wind sources.

Solar wind sets the speed used for the current sheet and the spirals. A faster wind gives a more open spiral and moves footpoints less far from the spacecraft's own longitude.

Fixed speed connects every spacecraft at that speed. Self-consistent lets each spacecraft find its own: starting from the slider speed, the footpoint's estimated wind speed is fed back into the spiral until it stops changing. The current sheet and extended lines keep the slider speed.

Log distance keeps the corona at true size and compresses the heliosphere, so the Sun and the fleet fit on one screen. True distance shows real proportions. At true distance the Sun is a speck seen from the fleet.

The menu

The menu button (☰) at the right of the top bar opens the layers, views and details for the day. It drops down below the button and turns into a × while open. Close it with the same button, L or Esc. On wide screens the page remembers whether you left it open. On phones it starts closed.

Each section folds, so you can keep open only what you use. The ? on a section links to its part of this guide.

  • This map lists the map time, the magnetogram file, the Carrington rotation, Earth's heliographic longitude and latitude, and the open magnetic flux.
  • Footpoints lists where each spacecraft's field line meets the photosphere, at the chosen wind speed or its self-consistent one. For each footpoint it gives the expansion factor fs, the distance to the nearest coronal-hole boundary (DCHB) and the wind speed estimated from them (how). Hover a row for the spread of footpoints and speeds over wind speed and source-surface height. In self-consistent mode, a * marks a spacecraft whose speed did not settle.
  • Save & share saves a snapshot, or copies a link or an embed code for exactly what you are looking at.

Saving snapshots

Save & share → Save PNG saves what you see at twice screen resolution, in the current theme. The image carries a title band and a legend. The title band gives the date, the magnetogram, the model, the wind speed and the open flux. It also gives the viewpoint of the saved view: the preset name, or "Custom view" after you rotate the scene, and the Carrington longitude and latitude it is seen from. Its Stonyhurst longitude, the angle from the Earth view, comes next, then the distance from Sun centre at true scale. Earth's L0 and B0 are listed separately for reference, so a view from anywhere can be placed relative to Earth. Spacecraft labels are drawn in the image at a size made for reading in a document. Save PDF puts the same picture on a single page sized to it, ready to drop into a document or a slide. The menu is never part of the image.

Downloading results

Save & share → Download results… saves the model behind the view as a zip. It holds the results in a NetCDF file, a header file (_header.json) that describes every variable, its units, the preprocessing and the model settings, a README, and Python scripts. The scripts draw the same pictures as the viewer, as PNG and PDF. They need only NumPy, SciPy and Matplotlib. Every zip has all the scripts, and a 3D zip also has the surface-map file. Names carry two dates, the model date and the day the files were saved in your browser: heliofield_model-2014-03-12_saved-2026-10-28_pfss.nc. Downloads are available for dates computed in your browser (source badge HMI CR …); daily products are solved on the server, and the page holds only their images.

Two photospheric maps. br_input is the archived map: the HMI rotation map averaged to 180 × 360, gaps set to zero and its mean removed, so the net flux is zero. The model never sees it at full detail. The browser expands it in spherical harmonics up to degree 80, with a Gaussian taper and without the monopole. That smooths it with a kernel of FWHM 3.8° and removes any leftover net flux (details). br_phot is that smoothed map, the model's actual boundary condition, and plot_surface.py shows it. The viewer's Sun, and plot_3d.py, show the input map.

Package versions. The scripts were tested with the versions in requirements-tested.txt. A new release of NumPy, SciPy or Matplotlib can break them, so they check for that. If a script fails, it fetches the latest published scripts from this site, checks them against download/manifest.json (SHA-256), keeps the old copies as .bak, and runs again. Each month the scripts are run against the newest package releases; when they pass, those versions become the tested ones, and when they fail, an issue is opened so they get fixed. python heliofield_update.py updates by hand, and HELIOFIELD_NO_UPDATE=1 turns the automatic update off.

Previews. Every zip has two images to look at first. One is the view on screen, as Save PNG makes it. The other shows the surface maps in the layout of plot_surface.py, drawn in the browser. The scripts make the full figures, as PNG and PDF.

The download uses the model on screen. Choosing the other 3D model switches the view to it first. compute_corona.py is the browser solver rewritten in NumPy. Given only the surface maps, it computes the 3D field, PFSS or PFSS + SCS, and writes a file in the format of a 3D download for plot_3d.py. The test suite checks that it reproduces the browser's results to within 10−5. It also lets you change the model: another source-surface height (--rss), another outer edge (--scs) or another harmonic degree (--lmax).

pip install -r requirements.txt
python plot_surface.py heliofield_model-2014-03-12_saved-2026-10-28_surface.nc
python compute_corona.py heliofield_model-2014-03-12_saved-2026-10-28_surface.nc --scs 21.5
python plot_3d.py heliofield_model-2014-03-12_computed-2026-10-28_scs.nc --theme light

The NetCDF file is in the classic format, which every NetCDF reader opens: xarray (engine="scipy"), netCDF4, Panoply, ParaView. Coordinates are Carrington; lengths are in solar radii and fields in gauss. The header file also records the camera, theme and wind speed on screen when the file was saved, so plot_3d.py starts from the same view.

With embed=1 the page shows the scene alone. A slim bar keeps the date controls, the menu button and a link to the full site. The menu's Save & share → Copy embed code writes the code for the current view:

        width="100%" height="560" style="border:0;border-radius:12px"
        loading="lazy" allowfullscreen></iframe>

Some useful embeds:

  • The Sun alone, turning: ?view=earth&layers=photo,closed,open,pil&rotate=1&embed=1&controls=0
  • Magnetic connectivity of the fleet: ?view=fleet&layers=photo,open,hcs,fleet,spiral&embed=1
  • The current sheet at true scale: ?view=fleet&layers=photo,hcs,fleet&scale=true&embed=1

Driving an embed from the host page

A page that embeds HelioField can change its date, wind speed, layers and view without reloading the frame. It does this with postMessage:

const frame = document.querySelector("iframe");
frame.contentWindow.postMessage(
  { type: "heliofield:set", date: "2014-03-12", v: 500, rss: 2.0, scs: 21.5, view: "earth" }, "*");

// HelioField answers after every change:
addEventListener("message", (e) => {
  if (e.data?.type === "heliofield:state") console.log(e.data.date, e.data.source);
});

Every field of the set message is optional. layers takes the same comma list as the URL, conn is "sc" or "fixed", rss sets the source-surface height for computed dates, and scs the outer edge of the current-sheet model (0 for PFSS alone). Either one alone re-solves the date on screen. The reply carries date, v, rss, scs, conn, source, computed (true for a date computed in the browser) and obstime. A host showing any date since 2010, such as HelioFleet, can pass it directly. Embeds and host messages compute past dates without asking; only the page itself shows the confirmation.

Scripts and automated agents

HelioField can be used without the page. That suits scripts, notebooks and automated assistants asked to show the Sun's coronal field for a date.

  • Links. Every view is a URL built from the parameters above: ?date=2014-03-12&view=earth, ?date=latest&view=fleet&layers=photo,hcs,fleet,spiral&scale=true. A link can be shared, embedded, or opened by a browser tool and captured. Add embed=1 so a past date computes without the confirmation dialog.
  • Figures without a browser. The download scripts can fetch the inputs for any date from this site themselves, then solve the same model as the page and write the figures: 
    pip install numpy scipy matplotlib

python compute_corona.py --date 2020-05-08 --scs 21.5 --plot --view fleet
    This writes the 3D view and the surface maps as PNG and PDF, and the results as NetCDF. The views are home, earth, north and fleet; --rss, --scs, --theme and --v work as on the page.
  • Descriptions for machines. llms.txt is a plain-text summary for automated agents (the llms.txt convention): what HelioField does, ready-made links, the scripts, the data files and the limits to mention. api.json lists the same parameters, scripts and data endpoints as JSON. Both are linked from the head of every page.
  • Data. The daily products (data/<date>/meta.json), the HMI rotation maps, the fleet positions and the solar-cycle series are static JSON and binary files on this site, readable from any program. api.json gives their addresses and formats.

Methods

Overview

Each day a job takes one full-Sun magnetogram and extrapolates it into a model of the coronal magnetic field. It traces field lines through that model and finds where the field changes sign at the model's outer boundary. It also places the planets and spacecraft in the same frame and follows each one's field line back to the Sun. The browser only draws the result. Everything heavy is computed ahead of time.

Magnetogram → PFSS extrapolation → traced field lines and neutral line → spacecraft connectivity → static files read by this page.

Magnetograms

The model's lower boundary is a synoptic map of the photospheric radial field. A synoptic map is assembled from many days of observations as the Sun rotates, so it covers every longitude. Its newest part is the longitude facing Earth. The data get older towards the east. The longitudes just behind the east limb were last seen nearly a full rotation, about four weeks, earlier.

GONG

The normal source is the GONG zero-point-corrected synoptic map (mrzqs). GONG updates it hourly. The build uses the last map of the day. These maps are 360 × 180 pixels, uniform in Carrington longitude and in the sine of latitude. The zero-point correction removes a small offset that would otherwise bias the open flux.

SDO/HMI fallback

The automated build runs on a cloud server that GONG's archive does not answer. When GONG cannot be reached, the build uses the SDO/HMI daily-updated synoptic frame from JSOC instead. It does so only when that frame is from the day being built. The HMI frame is averaged 2 × 2 onto the same grid as GONG, so both sources go through an identical model. The two agree in longitude to within a pixel, but their field strengths differ in detail. A day's source is shown by the badge beside the date.

Preparation

Before extrapolation, the map's mean is removed. A potential field cannot carry net magnetic monopole, and a synoptic map rarely balances exactly.

PFSS model

The coronal field comes from the potential-field source-surface (PFSS) model. It assumes there are no electric currents between the photosphere and a spherical source surface. With no currents the field is the gradient of a scalar potential, and that potential satisfies Laplace's equation:

∇ × B = 0  ⇒  B = −∇Φ,    ∇²Φ = 0   for  R☉ ≤ r ≤ Rss

Two boundary conditions close the problem:

  • At the photosphere, the radial field matches the magnetogram: Br(R☉, θ, φ) = Bobs(θ, φ).
  • At the source surface, the field is purely radial: Φ(Rss, θ, φ) = 0. This mimics the solar wind, which drags the field out radially beyond a few solar radii.

The source surface sits at Rss = 2.5 R☉, the conventional value, unless you change it for a past date. Field lines that reach it are open. All others close back on the Sun.

The equation is solved with sunkit-magex, the SunPy-affiliated successor to pfsspy. It uses a finite-difference scheme on a grid uniform in ln r, cos θ and φ. The horizontal grid matches the magnetogram, and there are 40 radial shells. One day's solve and trace takes about ten seconds.

Choosing the source-surface height

Rss is the model's one free parameter, and 2.5 R☉ is a convention rather than a measurement. Lowering it lets more of the field reach the boundary:

  • Open flux rises. On the March 2014 rotation map (CR 2148) it is 2.8 × 1022 Mx at 1.8 R☉ and 1.0 × 1022 Mx at 3.0 R☉.
  • Coronal holes grow. Lines that closed under a high boundary now open, so the footprints spread and new small holes appear.
  • The neutral line warps. A low source surface keeps more of the photospheric structure, so the current sheet reaches higher latitudes. A high one leaves mostly the dipole, so the sheet flattens towards the equator.
  • Spacecraft connections move. Footpoints can jump to another coronal hole when its open field changes.

No single height fits all conditions. Comparisons with in-situ field polarity have favoured lower heights near solar minimum (Lee et al. 2011), and with Parker Solar Probe close to the Sun, about 2.0 R☉ (Badman et al. 2020). Moving the slider and watching which features persist is a simple test of how much a result depends on this choice. The footpoint spread layer does the same for the connections, at 0.5 R☉ either side of the chosen height. The open-field footprints for past dates normally come from the full sunkit-magex solution at 2.5 R☉. At any other height they come from the browser's own solution, traced the same way on a 1° grid.

Open flux

The open flux is the unsigned radial flux through the source surface:

Φopen = Rss2 ∮ |Br(Rss, θ, φ)| dΩ

It is a standard summary of how much field the Sun opens into the heliosphere. PFSS with GONG or HMI input commonly underestimates the open flux measured in situ near 1 au. Treat it as a relative measure from day to day, not an absolute one.

Schatten current-sheet model

PFSS forces the field to be radial at the source surface, but it keeps the latitude structure of the field there. Beyond it, |Br| r2 stays strong over the poles and weak near the neutral line. Ulysses found the opposite: far from the Sun, |Br| r2 hardly depends on latitude (Smith & Balogh 1995). The open flux spreads out sideways until the magnetic pressure evens out, and it is separated into the two polarities by a thin current sheet. The Schatten current-sheet model (Schatten 1972) builds that state while keeping the field potential everywhere except in the sheets. It is the outer part of the WSA model (Arge & Pizzo 2000).

  1. PFSS inside. The PFSS solution is used up to a cusp surface at Rcp = 0.92 Rss (2.3 R☉ for the default 2.5). The cusp surface sits below the source surface, where the PFSS field is not yet forced radial. This reduces the kink in field lines at the interface (McGregor et al. 2008). A line that reaches the cusp surface is open.
  2. Reorient. On the cusp surface, take |Br|. The field now points outward everywhere, so there are no neutral lines to anchor closed loops.
  3. Solve outward. Expand |Br(Rcp)| in spherical harmonics up to degree 80, with the same taper and including the monopole term. The potential field outside the sphere that vanishes at infinity is then
    Br(r, θ, φ) = Σ alm (Rcp / r)l+2 Ylm(θ, φ),
    with the horizontal components from the same potential. The higher degrees fade quickly with distance, so the field tends to the monopole: radial, and even in latitude.
  4. Blend across the shell. Between the cusp surface and the source surface, both solutions exist. Their field directions differ, because the SCS field starts spreading the open flux sideways right above the cusp surface while the PFSS field is still nearly radial. On the HMI maps of 2014 and 2020, the difference is a median of 15–22°, and a field line traced straight from one model into the other bends sharply there. HelioField removes the kink by blending across the shell. The PFSS field, turned to point outward, gives way smoothly to the SCS field, from all PFSS at the cusp surface to all SCS at the source surface. Lines then turn gradually over the shell; the direction jump at the cusp surface drops to a median of 0.2°. A blend of two solutions does not keep ∇·B exactly zero. On the test maps it changes the unsigned flux in the shell by at most 0.8%. Beyond the source surface the field is pure SCS again.
  5. Restore the sign. Each field line takes the polarity of Br where it left the cusp surface. Lines from opposite polarities lie side by side above each cusp-surface neutral line, and the current sheet sits between them. HelioField traces the sheet directly, by following field lines outward from the cusp-surface neutral line.

The unsigned flux through every sphere outside the cusp surface is the same: the open flux shown is measured on the cusp surface. With SCS, the expansion factor fs is measured between the photosphere and the cusp surface, and a spacecraft connects to the model at its outer edge. Its Parker spiral then starts from the outer edge rather than from the source surface. The outer edge can be 5 R☉ (as in WSA), 10 R☉, or 21.5 R☉ (0.1 au, where heliospheric models such as ENLIL usually start). The SCS field is close to radial by then: on average within 0.6° at 21 R☉ on the test maps. Beyond the edge, the extended lines, the current sheet and the spirals continue as before.

On the HMI map at the 2020 minimum (CR 2230), PFSS |Br| r2 at 2.5 R☉ varies by a factor of 27 between 10° latitude bands. The SCS field at 21 R☉ varies by 3%.

SCS is still a static, current-free model outside the sheets, in the frame turning with the Sun. It does not include the solar wind's acceleration or stream interactions, and the field inside the outer edge is not wound by rotation. The Parker spiral starts at the outer edge, so lines continued beyond it (and the current sheet) turn there by the spiral angle: about 6° at 400 km/s at 21.5 R☉, and less at the lower edges.

Field lines

About 740 field lines are traced each day. Their starting points are chosen to show all of the corona fairly:

  • A set spread evenly over the Sun at 1.05 R☉, so that quiet regions are sampled.
  • A set drawn with probability weighted by |Br|1.5, so that active regions get detail.
  • A set spread evenly just inside the source surface, so that open field is shown wherever it reaches the boundary. Starting only from strong field would return almost nothing from coronal holes.

Each line is traced in both directions until it leaves the domain. A line is open if one end reaches the source surface. Its colour comes from the sign of the field at its photospheric end. Lines are resampled evenly along their length before they are stored.

Extending open lines into the heliosphere

PFSS stops at the source surface, where its outer boundary condition makes the field purely radial. Beyond it the model says nothing more, so no second solution is needed: the open field simply leaves the Sun along the radial direction it has at the boundary. The page continues each open line from its outer end in one of two ways.

  • Radial. The line goes straight out at the latitude and longitude where it left the source surface. This is the boundary condition taken literally. It would also be the field's shape if the solar wind were infinitely fast.
  • Parker spiral (default). The solar wind carries the radial field outward at the chosen speed v while the Sun rotates beneath it, so in the Sun's rotating frame each line winds back in longitude: φ(r) = φss − Ω (r − Rss) / v, at constant latitude. This is the same continuation used for the current sheet and for spacecraft connectivity, so the three agree. Lines of opposite polarity meet along the current sheet, and each spacecraft's connecting spiral lies among the extended lines of its own sector.

Lines traced on the server end at 0.98 Rss, where their outer seeds sit. Lines traced in the browser end at Rss. The field in that last shell is already all but radial, so the extension starts wherever each line ends. Like the current sheet, the extension is kinematic. It has no solar-wind acceleration, no stream interactions, no transients, and no change of latitude with distance.

Heliospheric current sheet

The heliospheric current sheet separates solar wind carrying outward field from wind carrying inward field. In PFSS its base is the neutral line, where Br = 0 on the source surface. The neutral line is found by contouring the source-surface field.

Beyond the source surface the sheet is drawn kinematically. Plasma leaving a point on the neutral line travels radially at the chosen speed v while the Sun turns beneath it. In the frame rotating with the Sun, that point's longitude falls behind with distance:

φ(r) = φ0 − Ω (r − Rss) / v,    Ω = 2π / 25.38 days

Here Ω is the Carrington sidereal rotation rate, and latitude stays constant along each strand. This is Parker's spiral, applied to every point of the neutral line. It is a picture of the sheet's shape. It is not a model of how the sheet warps, interacts or evolves in the solar wind.

Spacecraft connectivity

A spacecraft's magnetic footpoint is found in two steps.

  1. Ballistic mapping to the source surface. The spacecraft at distance rsc and longitude φsc is joined to the source surface along a Parker spiral at the chosen wind speed: φss = φsc + Ω (rsc − Rss) / v, at the spacecraft's latitude.
  2. PFSS mapping to the photosphere. From just inside that point, the model field line is traced down to the solar surface. Its end is the footpoint listed in the menu.

Footpoints are computed for wind speeds of 200 to 900 km/s in steps of 25, which covers the slider, the spread around it and the self-consistent mode. The uncertainty of a footpoint is large. The wind speed is rarely known to better than 100 km/s. For a spacecraft near 1 au, that alone shifts the footpoint by about 20 degrees of longitude in slow wind, and by about 5 degrees in fast wind. Close to the neutral line, a small shift can move the footpoint into the other polarity.

Spacecraft positions come from HelioFleet, which takes them from JPL Horizons. They are converted to the Carrington frame for the time of the magnetogram.

Solar-wind sources

A footpoint becomes a testable prediction once it says what kind of wind should arrive. HelioField gives three numbers for each one, in the spirit of the Wang–Sheeley–Arge (WSA) model.

  • Expansion factor. How much the flux tube widens between the photosphere and the source surface, compared with radial expansion. Flux conservation gives it from the field strength at the two ends of the traced line:
    fs = (|Bphot| / |Bss|) (R☉ / Rss)2
    Fast wind comes from the slowly expanding centres of large coronal holes (fs of a few); slow wind from strongly expanding tubes near their edges (Wang & Sheeley 1990).
  • Distance to the coronal-hole boundary (DCHB). The angle on the Sun from the footpoint to the nearest edge of its coronal hole. The holes are mapped by tracing a field line from every cell of a 1° grid just above the photosphere and marking it open or closed. The boundary is the set of midpoints between neighbouring open and closed cells, so DCHB is resolved to about half a degree.
  • Estimated speed. The two combine in the form used by Arge et al. (2003, 2004):
    v = 285 + 625 / (1 + fs)2/9 × [1 − 0.8 exp(−(DCHB / 2°)2)]3  km/s
    The coefficients are illustrative, not tuned. Operational versions fit them to observed wind for a given magnetogram source and resolution, and add a radial acceleration, so these speeds are a guide to the kind of wind, not a forecast.

Self-consistent connection. The ballistic mapping needs a speed, and the footpoint gives one. Starting from the slider speed, HelioField maps each spacecraft to its footpoint, takes the estimated speed there, rounds it to the 25 km/s grid and maps again, until the speed repeats. Near a coronal-hole edge the footpoint can jump between two sources whose speeds point back at each other. The speed then cycles; HelioField keeps the cycle member whose own estimated speed is closest to it, and marks the row with a *. A different starting speed can settle on a different source, which is itself a measure of the uncertainty.

Footpoint spread. The connection is mapped again for every wind speed within 100 km/s of the one used, on source surfaces at 2.0, 2.5 and 3.0 R☉, giving up to 27 footpoints per spacecraft. Wind speed moves the source-surface point in longitude; the source-surface height changes which field is open and where. For past dates the extra surfaces cost little, because the browser solution only re-evaluates the same harmonic coefficients.

Server and browser agree on where the connections land: on the test maps, footpoints are within about 1° (median), DCHB within 0.3° and the estimated speed within a few km/s; see Validation. The expansion factor differs more. fs depends on the photospheric field at the footpoint, which the browser smooths (see smoothing), so its fs is about half the server's. Because the speed depends on (1 + fs)2/9, a factor of two in fs moves it by at most about 15%.

Connectivity page

The connectivity page follows the in-situ spacecraft (Parker Solar Probe, Solar Orbiter, BepiColombo, STEREO-A and Earth with the L1 monitors) back to their sources for one date. It combines this model with HelioFleet's catalogue of observations and its instrument fields. The field is the HMI rotation map covering the date, solved in the browser with PFSS or PFSS + Schatten current sheet at the chosen source-surface height, as in the viewer. Positions are HelioField's daily positions at 12:00 UTC.

  • Connection. Each spacecraft is joined to the model's outer boundary (the source surface, or the outer edge of the current-sheet model) along a ballistic Parker spiral at the chosen speed, or at its footpoint's own WSA-type speed (self-consistent). It then follows a field line down to the photosphere, as described under spacecraft connectivity and solar-wind sources.
  • Source time. Plasma reaching a spacecraft at distance r left the outer boundary a travel time (r − Redge) / v earlier: about 4 days for 1 au at 400 km/s, hours for Parker Solar Probe near perihelion. The page gives this source time for each spacecraft.
  • Inside the current-sheet model. With SCS out to 21.5 R☉, Parker Solar Probe near perihelion is inside the model. It is then traced along the model field from its own position, with no Parker spiral, and its travel time is counted from the cusp surface. The 3D viewer and the download scripts do the same.
  • Polarity. The predicted interplanetary field polarity is the sign of the model field where the spiral meets the outer boundary. For PFSS this is Br just inside the source surface; with SCS, the sign carried along the current-sheet model's field. Agreement is the share of the footpoint spread (speeds ±100 km/s, source surfaces ±0.5 R☉) whose photospheric field has the same sign. A low agreement, or a small angle to the current sheet, means a sector boundary is near and the prediction is uncertain.
  • Polarity over a rotation. The same prediction day by day for 13 days either side, at the chosen speed and with this rotation's field, gives the expected sector structure.
  • Observations of the source region. HelioFleet's footprints of IRIS, Hinode/EIS, DKIST and Solar Orbiter (EUI-HRI, PHI-HRT, SPICE) are searched over the source-time window. That is the range of source times for speeds ±100 km/s around the one used, widened by 6 hours. For each observation, the footpoint and its spread are projected into the observation's view (Earth's, or Solar Orbiter's from its recorded position) at the observation's time. The observation is listed when its field contains the footpoint or lies within 5° of the spread. The projections follow SunPy's conventions and match SunPy to 0.0001″ (tests/test_connect.py).
  • Coronagraph fields. The model's open field and current sheet are projected onto the plane of the sky of each coronagraph or heliospheric imager whose spacecraft has a position on the date. Plane-of-sky radii are used for coronagraphs and elongation for wide-field imagers. Beyond the model's outer boundary, the current sheet and the open field are carried out as Parker spirals at the chosen speed. Structures are drawn brighter near the plane of the sky, where Thomson scattering makes them most visible. The fields of view (inner and outer limits, and the side for half-field imagers) are HelioFleet's.

Limits. The model is static over the rotation and current-free outside the sheets. The ballistic mapping ignores acceleration, stream interactions and coronal mass ejections, so a footpoint can be off by 10° or more; the spread shows how much. The search finds only observations in HelioFleet's catalogue, and the footprints carry the pointing recorded with each observation. Dates newer than the latest HMI rotation map use the nearest map, as the page says.

Coordinates and scale

The whole scene is in the Carrington frame. It is fixed to the rotating Sun, with z along the solar rotation axis and x at Carrington longitude 0. The field therefore stays still from day to day, and the planets and spacecraft move around it. Earth's position in this frame is given by L0 and B0, its heliographic longitude and latitude.

In log-distance mode, distances out to the source surface are true. Beyond it, they are compressed:

r′ = Rss + k ln[1 + (r − Rss) / k],   k = 2.5 Rss   for  r > Rss

This puts 1 au at about 25 R☉. The compression starts with slope 1 at the source surface, so field lines that cross it keep their shape on screen. Spirals, the current sheet and the extended field lines are computed at true distance and compressed afterwards, so their longitudes are unchanged.

Past dates

A date without a daily product is computed in your browser. The page cannot compute on a server, because it is a static site. The GONG and JSOC archives do not allow other websites to read their files directly. So HelioField keeps its own small archive of inputs.

  • One HMI map per Carrington rotation since May 2010 (hmi.Synoptic_Mr_small from JSOC). Each is averaged to the same 180 × 360 grid as GONG, which comes to about 85 kB per rotation. The 2012 rotations exist at JSOC only at full resolution and are averaged down from those. The two paths give identical maps where both exist.
  • Daily positions of Earth, the planets and the spacecraft at 12:00 UTC, converted to the Carrington frame from HelioFleet's ephemerides. STEREO-B is shown until contact was lost on 1 October 2014.

For a chosen date the page takes the rotation whose time span contains noon of that day. It downloads that map and solves the field in a background thread. The same method is used as for daily products, with a spherical-harmonic solution up to degree 80, tapered (see below), on a 36 × 90 × 180 grid. It then traces the same seed pattern of field lines, contours the neutral line and maps the spacecraft connections, with coronal holes on three source surfaces for the source diagnostics. On a laptop this takes a few seconds. Moving to another day in the same rotation only re-traces the spacecraft connections.

Smoothing of the magnetogram

The archived map is already balanced: the HMI rotation map is averaged to 180 × 360, gaps are set to zero, and the mean is removed, so the net flux is zero. The browser then expands it in spherical harmonics up to degree 80, without the monopole. Each degree l is weighted by a Gaussian taper, wl = exp[−l(l+1)/(2 × 422)]. This is the same as smoothing the map with a Gaussian kernel of FWHM 3.8° before the solve. Cutting the series sharply instead, as the page did until October 2026 (at degree 40), leaves rings around strong active regions: the smoothing kernel then has side lobes of 13% of its peak, against 2.6% with the taper. Those rings were partly closing field that should be open. Without them, the model opens 14–32% more of the photosphere than sunkit-magex does on the full 1° map. The source-surface field, open flux, neutral line and spacecraft connections hardly change. The smoothed map is the one the solution uses; downloads include it as br_phot, next to the archived map, br_input.

Checked against the server pipeline, the browser solution agrees closely: source-surface field correlation 0.9998, neutral line within a fraction of a degree, open flux within 2%, and spacecraft footpoints within about 1°. The full comparison is under Validation.

The test suite repeats this on HMI rotation maps from three phases of the cycle; see Validation.

A rotation map is assembled over about 27 days, so a computed date shows the field around that date. A daily product shows the field as of that day.

Open-field footprints

A field line is traced from the centre of every cell of a 1° grid just above the surface (1.001 R☉), with rows equally spaced in sine latitude so every cell has the same area. A line that reaches the source surface is open, and its sign is the polarity of its footpoint; a line that comes back down is closed. That is 64,800 lines per map, traced with sunkit-magex on the same PFSS solution as the daily product.

  • Daily products have their own footprints, made by the nightly job from the same magnetogram (data/<date>/open.png).
  • Past dates, and days without their own map, use the footprints of the HMI rotation map that covers the date (data/history/open/NNNN.png). The info panel says which one is shown.

For past dates the field lines are solved in your browser from the smoothed map (spherical harmonics up to degree 80, tapered), while the footprints at the default height come from the full sunkit-magex solution of the same map. The two agree to within a cell or two at hole boundaries, so a drawn open line occasionally starts just outside a patch.

Solar-cycle series

The series page reduces each HMI rotation map since May 2010 to a few numbers, solving it with sunkit-magex at the daily settings (source surface 2.5 R☉, 40 radial shells). A new point is added when JSOC publishes the next rotation map, about once a month. Each chart draws every rotation and its 13-rotation running mean.

  • Open flux: the unsigned flux through the source surface, the sum of |Br| over its area.
  • Open area: the share of the photosphere covered by open-field footprints.
  • Current-sheet tilt: the highest latitude the source-surface neutral line reaches in each hemisphere, the quantity the Wilcox Solar Observatory reports for its own maps (Hoeksema 1984). Sign changes between neighbouring cells are interpolated in latitude. The grid's highest row is at 84°, so a neutral line that crosses the pole reads 84°.
  • Polar field: the mean photospheric Br of all cells poleward of 60° in each hemisphere.

The numbers are for this model on these maps, not for the Sun as such. The open flux from PFSS is typically about half of what spacecraft measure at 1 au, a long-standing difference known as the open flux problem (Linker et al. 2017); it depends on the source-surface height and on the polar fields. WSO's tilt uses line-of-sight maps and other source-surface heights, so its values differ from these by several degrees, most near maximum. The polar means are dominated by fields seen at a grazing angle, and the annual swing of B0 by ±7° hides each pole for part of the year.

The solar-cycle panel in the viewer

On wide screens the viewer shows the four series in a panel at the left; View → Solar-cycle panel or C shows or hides it, and on phones it opens as a sheet at the bottom. A dashed line marks the date the viewer is showing, and a ring on each series marks the rotation map that covers it; a daily product newer than the latest rotation map is marked past the last point. The marker follows every change of date. Click a point on any chart to open the middle of that rotation in the viewer, computed in your browser as for any past date. ↗ opens the full series page.

Colours and accessibility

The colours were chosen by calculation, not by eye. Each one was checked in both themes against the background it is drawn on.

RoleDark themeLight theme

Neutral line and current sheet#d7506e rose#9c113f
  • Colour-vision deficiency. Every pair of field colours stays distinct under simulated protanopia and deuteranopia (Machado et al. 2009). The worst pair is separated by ΔE 9.8 in the dark theme and 15.6 in the light, where 8 is the target (OKLab × 100). Tritanopia is also checked.
  • Normal vision. Every pair is at least ΔE 17 apart.
  • Contrast. Every line colour has at least 3:1 contrast against its background, the WCAG 2.1 requirement for graphical objects (success criterion 1.4.11). Text meets WCAG AA (at least 4.5:1) in both themes.
  • Spacecraft use a fixed categorical order, stepped in lightness to reach 3:1 on the light background. Every marker carries a text label, so identity never rests on colour alone.

The surface field uses one diverging scale in both themes. Gold for outward field and cerulean for inward field sit at equal lightness on either side of a neutral silver. It is paler than the line colours, so a line stays visible where it crosses a region of its own polarity. Sign is read from hue, and strength from how far the colour moves from silver.

Validation

The browser solver is tested independently of the page, from Python, by a test suite in the repository (tests/). The suite runs js/pfss.js itself under Node.js, so the code it checks is the code the page runs. Anyone can rerun it. It is not part of routine updates; it runs by hand or from the repository's Validation workflow.

pip install -r requirements.txt -r requirements-test.txt
python -m pytest tests -v                 # everything, two to three minutes
python -m pytest tests -v -m "not slow"   # exact solutions only, seconds

Exact solutions. The associated Legendre functions match SciPy to round-off. A dipole on the photosphere has a closed-form PFSS solution, and the solver reproduces it: the field within 2% everywhere between the photosphere and the source surface, traced field lines that keep the dipole's flux function constant to 1%, open flux within 1%, and a neutral line on the equator to 0.6°. The speed formula matches the Python pipeline's to round-off. The harmonic analysis of a single high-degree term leaks about 4% of its amplitude into other degrees, because the latitude integral uses the map's own uniform rows rather than Gauss–Legendre points. The test keeps that limit in view.

Against sunkit-magex. Three HMI rotation maps from the archive, one near the cycle-24 maximum (CR 2148, March 2014), one at the 2019–20 minimum (CR 2230, May 2020) and one in cycle 25 (CR 2300, July 2025), are solved by both codes at a source surface of 2.5 R☉. The current-sheet model (tests/test_scs.py) is checked in three ways.

  • Dipole. The SCS field of a dipole has a series solution, |cos θ| expanded in Legendre polynomials. The browser's field matches it within 2% from 2.6 to 21 R☉, and its current sheet lies on the equator at every height.
  • Against Python. For CR 2148 and CR 2230, an independent SCS field is built in Python from the sunkit-magex PFSS field at the cusp surface. At 3 to 20 R☉, Br correlates at 0.9996 or better with a median difference under 1.7%.
  • Physical checks. Beyond the source surface, the unsigned flux through spheres out to 21 R☉ is constant within 0.1%. Inside the transition shell it differs by at most 0.8%. Across the cusp surface, the field direction changes by a median of 0.2°. |Br| r2 at 21 R☉ varies by less than 4% between latitude bands. At 3° either side of the traced current sheet, the polarities are opposite at 100% (2020) and 94% (2014) of the points; near maximum the sheet folds, and some points fall on another fold.

The field strength agrees to about 1% (median) everywhere above 1.1 R☉. At the photosphere the browser's |B| is 30–60% smaller in median, because the browser smooths the map (FWHM 3.8°) while sunkit-magex works on the full 1° map. That one difference explains the rest. The expansion factor scales with the photospheric field, so it is about half the server's. Open area depends on the smallest closed regions, so the browser opens 14–32% more.

The field that the smoothing removes is the small-scale network field of the quiet Sun. It closes within a few hundredths of a solar radius: the gap is 12–26% at 1.02 R☉ and about 4% at 1.05 R☉. So it changes what is read at the surface itself, the expansion factor and the smallest closed loops, and leaves the corona above unchanged. Any PFSS model has this dependence on the map's resolution, and WSA-type speed formulas are calibrated for a particular map and resolution. The expansion factor takes the photospheric field from the harmonic series itself, not the 2° grid, which would lose a further 4–6% at the surface. The daily products, solved by sunkit-magex on the full map, keep the full-resolution values. The speed depends on (1 + fs)2/9, so it barely moves. The values come from the version of each code that the tests ran. Each run writes them to cache/validation/, and the tests fail if they leave these ranges.

Limitations

  • No currents. PFSS cannot represent sheared or twisted fields, such as those in filament channels or before eruptions. Active-region loops are less twisted than in reality.
  • Static, potential models. PFSS and SCS hold no currents outside the current sheets and no solar-wind dynamics. The current-sheet model is available only for dates computed in the browser.
  • A spherical source surface. The real boundary between closed and open field is neither spherical nor at one height. Its height changes the open flux and the shape of the neutral line; for past dates it can be varied, but daily products use 2.5 R☉.
  • Old data away from Earth. A synoptic map shows some longitudes as they were up to about four weeks earlier. New active regions there are missing until they rotate into view.
  • Poorly observed poles. The polar fields are seen at a grazing angle. They strongly influence the open field, and so the current sheet.
  • Constant, radial solar wind. The spirals and current sheet ignore acceleration, stream interactions and transients such as coronal mass ejections.
  • The HMI fallback lags. The HMI frame is typically several hours old when it is published.
  • Source diagnostics are indicative. The speed formula's coefficients are illustrative, the wind is assumed to travel at constant speed from the source surface, and the expansion factor depends on the magnetogram and the model's resolution. Footpoints near a coronal-hole edge, where slow wind comes from, are the most sensitive to all of this.
  • Footprints are the model's coronal holes. They follow the open field of PFSS and are not compared with EUV images; the model typically opens a smaller area than the dark regions seen in EUV.
  • Past dates use rotation maps. They show the field averaged over the rotation, not that day's map, and the browser solution smooths the map to a resolution of about 4° (FWHM 3.8°).
